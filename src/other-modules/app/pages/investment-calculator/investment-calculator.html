Before You Run the Numbers

  • Age 45 is a "gate." Clear it in reasonable health and your expected lifespan already jumps past the national average — to ~79 (men) / ~83 (women).
  • Heart disease is the #1 killer in the U.S., and not moving is its biggest controllable cause.
  • Staying active works: the most-fit people see ~80% lower mortality. Lifelong healthy habits push average lifespan to ~87–93, and living well past 90 is a realistic target.
  • 1 in 10 healthy people live to their mid-90s — a genuinely great outcome on its own.
  • Consider your overall life expectancy, your active life expectancy, and what you want to be doing in retirement (e.g. staying home or travelling the world) when running the numbers below.

Learn More About These Numbers

TL;DR

  1. Pay off all debt. Debt interest rates are often far higher than you'll ever earn investing — credit cards currently average over 20% APR.
  2. Open a High-Yield Savings Account (currently 4%+ APY) and build up to 6 months of living expenses in it.
  3. Keep up to 3 months of living expenses in your checking account.
  4. Invest the rest.
  • Treat money once it's invested as effectively deleted — pretend you can't access it, and almost never withdraw from it. Selling early means locking in losses during a downturn, missing out on the recovery, and often real costs (capital gains tax, early-withdrawal penalties on retirement accounts). This is exactly why the checklist above puts 3 months in checking and 6 months in a HYSA before investing — that cash is your access to money for emergencies, so you're never tempted to raid the investments.
  • One of the biggest levers in FI/RE (Financial Independence, Retire Early): get as much money invested as early as possible, then leave it alone for years. Growth compounds on your whole balance, so it's tiny at first and huge later — a $2M balance growing 10%/year gains ~$200k that single year alone. Time in the market does most of the work — see the Growth Over Time chart above.
  • If your employer offers a 401(k) match, capture the full match before investing anywhere else — it's extra money on top of your own contributions, not counted in this calculator's numbers (see CNBC on employer 401(k) matches).
  • Lowball your inputs (contributions, returns) when estimating how long this takes — better to finish a few years earlier than expected than to count on an optimistic number and come up short.
  • Invest in the S&P 500 only — skip individual stocks.
  • Every $1M invested generates roughly $32k/year, post-tax, forever, at an indefinite withdrawal rate (see Morningstar's withdrawal-rate research above).
  • Spending often jumps in the active "go-go" years right after retiring — more travel, more free time to fill — before easing off later and rising again for healthcare in old age (researchers call this the "retirement spending smile"). It's not a simple one-way increase, so budget some cushion for that early stretch.
  • Renting is currently cheaper than buying in essentially every major U.S. metro on a monthly basis, thanks to mortgage rates near 6.6% — but that's a 2026 snapshot, not a permanent rule. Selling later also costs real money: realtor commissions plus closing costs typically run 8–10% of the sale price (more with repairs or moving), which comes straight out of any equity you built. That's why buying only tends to pay off once you've stayed long enough to recoup it — nationally that break-even point is roughly 5–7 years, though it swings wildly by market (as little as ~3.5 years in affordable metros, decades in places like San Francisco). Moving sooner than that usually means renting would've come out ahead (see Empower's 2026 rent-vs-buy breakdown, Zillow's break-even research).
  • Be frugal: avoid cars newer than 10 years or pricier than $15–20k, and limit eating out to once or twice a month.
  • Watch for lifestyle creep — spending quietly rising to match every raise. Keep your cost of living flat as income grows.
  • Adding a partner's income often multiplies your savings rate 2–4x or more, not just 2x — their income skips straight to savings once the first income already covers the household's cost of living. Example: a $70k earner saving ~$8k/year could jump to ~$50k/year saved after adding a $50k partner income, since nearly all of that second income becomes pure savings.
  • California and New York consistently rank among the worst states for income-to-cost-of-living balance — both sit near the top of overall cost-of-living indexes, and New York ranks dead last on housing affordability specifically (see Visual Capitalist's 2026 cost-of-living map).
  • Everywhere else is basically fine — most places land on a pretty similar cost-of-living-to-income ratio, big cities included. So it doesn't really matter where you live, as long as it's outside California and New York.
  • Investment income doesn't reduce or disqualify Social Security — only wages from a job count against SS before full retirement age, and even that limit disappears entirely once you reach it. So at the right age, your portfolio withdrawals and your SS benefit stack — the average Social Security check (~$2,071/month, or ~$25k/year, in 2026) comes on top of whatever you've built, like a bonus, not a trade-off. (Source on the earnings rule: SSA — How work affects your benefits.)

Income vs. Spending, By Income Bracket

A single national average gets pulled way up by a small number of very high earners — it doesn't reflect what a typical household actually makes or spends. Split out by income quintile instead, after-tax income doesn't comfortably cover typical spending until well into the upper brackets.

That negative number for most brackets isn't hypothetical, and it doesn't just disappear — it gets filled with credit card balances, auto loans, or savings that never get the chance to build up, which is a big part of why household debt is so widespread. If your own numbers look like one of the brackets running a deficit, the two moves that matter most are the ones actually in your control: push to upskill, retrain, or switch fields into higher-paying work as soon as you reasonably can, and treat your cost of living as something you actively manage down rather than a fixed fact of life. Neither is easy, but together they're close to the only realistic path to a genuinely healthy financial future — whether that arrives before age 67 or, for a lot of people starting from behind, sometime well after it.

How Long You'll Actually Live

U.S. life expectancy at birth is about 78.4 years overall — 75.8 for men, 81.1 for women (CDC, 2023). But that number is pulled down hard by deaths concentrated in childhood and young adulthood (accidents, overdoses, violence). Once you clear that stretch, the math resets: Social Security's own actuarial tables give a 45-year-old man roughly 34 more years on average (to ~79) and a 45-year-old woman roughly 38 more (to ~83) — already above the birth-cohort number, before you've done anything to earn it. Call that the "age 45 gate": survive to there in reasonable shape, and the odds already favor a longer run than the headline stat implies.

Heart disease has been the #1 cause of death in the U.S. since 1950 — 680,909 people in 2023 alone — and the single biggest modifiable driver behind it is simply not moving. The World Health Organization ranks physical inactivity as the 4th-leading risk factor for death worldwide, behind an estimated 30% of ischaemic heart disease cases, yet only about 1 in 4 U.S. adults gets enough aerobic and strength activity to meet the CDC's own guidelines. The upside runs deep: a Cleveland Clinic study of over 122,000 patients found no ceiling on the benefit of cardio fitness — people in the top fitness bracket had roughly an 80% lower death rate than the least-fit group, with gains still climbing at the high end. Basic strength tracks the same way — a Harvard study of 1,100+ firefighters found the ones who could do 40+ pushups had a 96% lower rate of cardiovascular disease over the following decade than those who could do fewer than 10.

Put together: staying active isn't just about living longer, it's about staying capable — those fitness numbers keep paying off well into your 70s, with no observed drop-off. A 34-year Harvard study tracking over 123,000 adults found that people who kept all five core habits (never smoking, normal weight, 30+ minutes of daily activity, a healthy diet, moderate alcohol) had an average life expectancy at age 50 of 87 for men and 93 for women — roughly a decade longer than people who kept none of them. A separate study of 821 middle-aged Norwegian men found that among non-smokers with high physical fitness, about half (48.8%) lived to 85, versus 27.9% of non-smokers with low fitness. Living to 90 isn't the national median, but it's a realistic target for someone who stays active and never smokes — a decades-long Finnish cohort study found 51% of men with none of the major cardiovascular risk factors (smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, obesity, high blood sugar) at midlife lived to 90, versus just 7% of those stacking five of them.

What about the very top? Social Security's own actuarial data puts the "top 10%" threshold — the age only 1 in 10 people live past — at around 95–96 for a man starting from his early 60s, and somewhat higher for a woman, not 100. Triple digits stay rare even in that lucky decile: among 65-year-olds today, Social Security projects only about 1.5% of men and 3.5% of women will live past age 100. So an active, health-conscious life buys real odds at your late 80s/90s and a shot at your late 90s if you land in that top decile — but 100 is still the exception, not something to plan your money around. The investing math on this page assumes you're around to spend it — this is the other half of that plan.

Why index funds?

Most individual investors can't reliably beat the market, and fees compound against you over decades. A fund that tracks the S&P 500 gives you ownership in roughly 500 of the largest U.S. companies for a fraction of a percent in annual fees — the "buy the whole market and hold" approach behind the Bogleheads investing philosophy.

Sources: SEC Investor.gov — Index Funds, Bogleheads Wiki — Three-Fund Portfolio

Why 10% by default?

Since 1926, the S&P 500 has returned about 10% a year on average before inflation (roughly 6.5–7% after inflation). Any single year can swing far above or below that — this is a very long-run average, not a guarantee.

Source: officialdata.org — S&P 500 Returns Since 1926

Why does this default to 3% inflation?

That's roughly the long-run U.S. average, and it's what turns the "Ending Balance" stat into something you can actually judge — $2M thirty years from now sounds huge, but in today's purchasing power it's a lot less. Contributions are kept flat on purpose rather than assumed to grow with raises: the TL;DR above already advises lowballing your inputs, and a raise schedule isn't something you can count on in advance.

Why a ~3.5% withdrawal rate instead of the famous 4%?

The "4% rule" comes from William Bengen's 1994 research and the 1998 Trinity Study, both of which tested a 30-year retirement — not an indefinite one. If you want a portfolio to fund withdrawals forever without shrinking, you generally need to withdraw a bit less. Morningstar's own updated retirement-income research has put a sustainable starting rate as low as 3.3% in some years (3.9% in its most recent estimate), depending on forward-looking return and inflation assumptions. At roughly 3.2–3.5%, every $1,000,000 invested supports about $32,000–$35,000 a year indefinitely — this calculator defaults to 3.5%.

Sources: Wikipedia — 4% Rule, Morningstar — Reevaluating the 4% Withdrawal Rule

What does this mean for retirement?

The "25x rule" used by the FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) community is just the 4% rule in reverse: save 25 times your annual expenses, and a 4% withdrawal should sustain you indefinitely. In practice, many people consider something in the $1M–$3M range enough to retire comfortably depending on their expenses and location, with $3M+ generally affording a very comfortable lifestyle almost anywhere. Your actual number depends entirely on your own spending, not a fixed dollar amount.

Source: Wikipedia — FIRE Movement

What if I don't want to leave money behind?

This calculator defaults to the perpetual-withdrawal model above — pull a small enough percentage that the balance keeps growing forever. That's the safe default if you want to leave money to heirs or just never risk running out. If you don't care about leaving an inheritance, author Bill Perkins makes the opposite case in Die With Zero: intentionally spend down your "peak" net worth during the years you're healthy enough to enjoy it, rather than dying with a large, unused balance. The tradeoff is longevity risk — spend too aggressively and you can outlive your money, so this only works if you've built in a real safety margin (or still have Social Security as a floor).

Source: Die With Zero by Bill Perkins

What about retiring in a cheaper country?

Every number on this page assumes U.S. costs. Retiring somewhere with a lower cost of living — geographic arbitrage — can stretch the same portfolio much further. South America, Southeast Asia, and Africa in particular tend to run cheapest, often by a wide margin, with Western Europe generally costing more. Requirements and costs vary enormously by country, so treat this as a direction to research, not a number to plan around.
